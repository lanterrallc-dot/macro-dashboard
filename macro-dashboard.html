The dollar against the majors

Equal-weighted across nine major currencies, rebased to 100 five years ago. Rising means a stronger dollar.

Is the dollar still acting like a haven?

Measured against everything except the dollar, do the yen and franc rise at the same times the dollar does? Both are havens, so this should sit comfortably above zero. Sustained readings below zero mean the dollar has stopped behaving like one — the single most consequential thing this page can tell you, and it is not visible in any dollar index.

Who is doing the moving

Shown as each currency's gain against the dollar, so positive means a weaker dollar. When one factor dominates, this column looks uniform.

Why the corner matters. A dollar falling while every currency moves on the same factor is a capital-flow story about American assets. The identical dominance reading with the dollar rising is a global risk event or a funding squeeze — the world reaching for dollars, which is the opposite diagnosis. Magnitude alone can't tell these apart, which is why the stress dashboard's unsigned dollar reading needed this companion.

What is missing, and it is the important half. The clearest warning on US assets is the dollar falling while Treasury yields rise, meaning investors demand more compensation to hold US paper and leave regardless. That needs yield data this feed doesn't carry. Check the ten-year against the dollar line here; if they diverge that way for more than a few weeks, it outranks everything on this page.

Read this slowly. Every measure here uses sixty-day windows deliberately. Capital reallocation happens over months, and short windows would fill the page with noise that looks like signal. A single week in a red corner is weather. A quarter is a decision being made.
